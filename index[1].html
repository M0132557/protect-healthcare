PROTECT

Patients and Providers for Reform, Oversight, Transparency, Equity, and Clinical Trust

Protecting people when healthcare institutions fail.

When health systems fail, patients and providers should not pay the price.

The Steward collapse exposed a second crisis: what happens when the financial structure meant to protect patients and clinicians from malpractice claims can no longer reliably do its job?

Understand the issueRead the reporting

One collapse. Two crises.

Steward's bankruptcy disrupted hospitals and communities. Its malpractice insurance structure created a separate, continuing problem for claimants and clinicians.

What is a captive?

A captive is an insurance company owned by the organization whose risks it insures. Captives are common risk-management tools, but governance, capitalization, liquidity and domicile matter.

Why reform?

The central policy question is simple: how should money reserved for patient claims and provider protection be safeguarded when a health system encounters financial distress?

Documented Case Study • 2010–Present

The Steward Health Care Crisis: A Multi-Source Timeline

Steward did not collapse overnight. This chronology combines investigative journalism with state records, federal proceedings, bankruptcy reporting, and Massachusetts health-care coverage. Boston Globe Spotlight remains an important source, but it is deliberately not the sole source. Each entry identifies where readers can examine the underlying reporting or public record.

Follow the evidence: corporate financing, hospital operations, patient care, government oversight, bankruptcy, hospital transitions, and the malpractice aftermath developed on overlapping tracks. Source badges below link directly to reporting or government records.
2010 • Steward is created from Caritas Christi
Steward Health Care was formed after Cerberus Capital Management acquired the six-hospital Caritas Christi system. The transaction was promoted as a way to stabilize struggling community hospitals and preserve access to care.
WBURBoston Globe Spotlight
2011–2015 • Expansion, closures, and early financial warnings
Steward expanded in Massachusetts, but warning signs emerged. WBUR, citing a 2015 Attorney General monitoring report, reported a roughly $75 million operating loss in 2014 and concerns about Steward's debt. Quincy Medical Center closed after Steward had previously committed to keep it open, while other services were reduced or eliminated at facilities including Carney and Morton.
WBURBoston Globe Spotlight
2016 • The $1.25 billion Medical Properties Trust sale-leaseback
Steward sold hospital real estate to Medical Properties Trust and leased the properties back. The transaction generated capital that supported expansion but also created long-term rent obligations. Both WBUR and Globe Spotlight later identified the MPT relationship as central to understanding Steward's financial trajectory.
WBURBoston Globe Spotlight
2017–2018 • Rapid national expansion
Steward acquired IASIS Healthcare and expanded far beyond Massachusetts, ultimately operating hospitals across multiple states. The company moved its headquarters from Boston to Dallas in 2018.
Boston Globe SpotlightWBUR Timeline
2019–2021 • TRACO moves offshore; ownership changes
Steward's captive malpractice insurer, TRACO, was moved from the Cayman Islands to Panama. Separately, Cerberus exited Steward through a transaction supported by MPT financing. Globe Spotlight reported a $111 million distribution to Steward owners after the restructuring. These events later became important to debates over where malpractice assets were located and how money moved through the system.
Globe: TRACOGlobe: MPT
2019–2024 • Hospital-level deficiencies and resource concerns accumulate
Globe Spotlight's analysis of federal inspection data identified more than 300 deficiency findings at Steward hospitals over a five-year period. Other reporting documented unpaid vendors and shortages of basic supplies. These findings are important context, but individual events and allegations vary and should be evaluated through their underlying inspection records and reporting.
Boston Globe dataWBUR
October 2023 • St. Elizabeth's maternal death becomes a prominent patient-safety case
Globe Spotlight reported on the death of Sungida Rashid after childbirth at St. Elizabeth's and reported that an embolism coil that could have been used to control bleeding was unavailable after devices had been repossessed. Federal investigators later issued an immediate-jeopardy citation, according to the Globe's reporting.
Boston Globe Spotlight
December 2023–January 2024 • Steward's financial distress becomes a statewide crisis
New England Sinai was slated to close, MPT disclosed major unpaid rent, and Massachusetts officials began planning for the possibility of hospital failures. WBUR reported that Steward hospitals represented about 8% of Massachusetts hospital discharges and 10% of emergency-room visits, illustrating the potential systemwide impact.
WBURWBUR Timeline
Spring 2024 • Massachusetts alleges patient-safety risks amid financial deterioration
In bankruptcy-related filings described by WBUR, Massachusetts officials alleged that Steward had made large investor payouts while hospitals lacked resources for essential equipment and drugs. State monitors reported that Good Samaritan Medical Center was low on trauma supplies in April even though it was the region's trauma center. These were allegations made by the Commonwealth in court filings, not independent findings of criminal liability.
WBUR / Court filings
May 6, 2024 • Steward files Chapter 11
Steward filed for bankruptcy protection after months of escalating financial distress. The bankruptcy moved decisions about hospital sales, debts, leases, vendors, employees, and other liabilities into federal court in Texas.
WBURBoston Globe Spotlight
June–July 2024 • Clinicians and patients confront uncertainty
WBUR reported that clinicians and patients were leaving or avoiding Steward facilities amid uncertainty over staffing, services, and the bankruptcy. In late July, Steward announced plans to close Carney Hospital and Nashoba Valley Medical Center after saying no qualified bids had emerged.
WBUR: workforceWBUR: closures
July 31–August 31, 2024 • Carney and Nashoba Valley are approved for closure and shut down
A federal bankruptcy judge approved the closures. Both hospitals ultimately closed at the end of August, eliminating two community hospitals even as state officials worked to preserve the remaining Steward facilities.
WBURGlobe oversight timeline
September 2024 • Massachusetts commits substantial public support to preserve five hospitals
WBUR reported that Massachusetts expected to provide more than $489 million over three years to support the transition of Steward hospitals to new operators and maintain care. The bankruptcy court approved the hospital sales in September.
WBUR
September 2024 • Congress escalates its Steward investigation
After former CEO Ralph de la Torre did not appear pursuant to a Senate HELP Committee subpoena, the committee voted 20-0 to advance civil enforcement and criminal contempt resolutions. On September 25, the Senate unanimously adopted the criminal contempt resolution. The contempt action concerned failure to comply with the congressional subpoena; it was not a criminal adjudication of responsibility for Steward's collapse.
U.S. Senate HELPSenate vote
Fall 2024 • Five Massachusetts hospitals transition to new operators
St. Elizabeth's and Good Samaritan transitioned to Boston Medical Center; Saint Anne's and Morton to Brown University Health; and Holy Family's campuses to Lawrence General Hospital. Steward's operating presence in Massachusetts largely ended, but the financial and malpractice consequences continued.
Massachusetts official recordWBUR
Late 2024 • The TRACO malpractice problem emerges as a second crisis
Globe Spotlight reported that TRACO listed about $3.5 million in cash while 517 malpractice claims were pending or settled but unpaid, and that Steward's lawyers estimated remaining claims could cost as much as $200 million. The issue shifted attention from keeping hospitals open to whether patients and clinicians could rely on legacy malpractice coverage.
Boston Globe Spotlight
2026 • Federal accountability questions remain unresolved
In August 2026, the bipartisan leaders of the Senate HELP Committee asked the Justice Department for an update on the 2024 criminal-contempt referral involving de la Torre. Their letter demonstrates that federal scrutiny of the Steward collapse continued well after the Massachusetts hospitals changed hands.
U.S. Senate HELP • Aug. 2026
Ongoing • The hospitals changed hands, but legacy liabilities did not simply disappear
The Steward story now has a second chapter: unpaid or disputed malpractice obligations, tail-coverage concerns for former clinicians, litigation over responsibility, and policy questions about captives, reserves, hospital liability, executive accountability, and provider protections. That continuing aftermath is the focus of the coalition's nine policy remedies.

Why the chronology matters

The crisis developed over more than a decade. Financing decisions, regulatory oversight, hospital operations, staffing and supplies, bankruptcy, and malpractice coverage are related but distinct issues. The timeline is intended to let readers examine those events before considering the proposed policy response.

This timeline distinguishes reporting, government allegations, official actions, and adjudicated outcomes. Links are provided so readers can examine the underlying sources. Inclusion of a source does not imply that the source endorses this coalition or its policy proposals.

The Second Crisis

The ongoing medical malpractice issue

517

The Boston Globe reported that TRACO listed 517 pending or settled-but-unpaid malpractice claims as of August 2024.

$3.5M

The Globe reported TRACO listed approximately $3.5 million in cash at that time.

Up to $200M

The Globe reported Steward's lawyers estimated remaining malpractice claims could cost as much as $200 million to resolve.

The core problem: malpractice coverage is supposed to protect injured patients and the clinicians who rely on that coverage. A hospital bankruptcy can therefore create consequences extending years beyond the sale or closure of a facility.

For patients

A successful malpractice claim has limited practical value if the responsible coverage lacks accessible assets to pay a settlement or judgment.

For clinicians

When expected institutional coverage is disputed or insufficient, clinicians may face uncertainty about defense costs, tail exposure and personal liability.

For regulators

The Steward experience raises questions about reserve requirements, related-party transactions, captive governance, transparency and where malpractice assets may be domiciled.

For every health system

This is broader than one company. Captives are widely used in health care. The policy challenge is to preserve their legitimate risk-management benefits while protecting the assets intended for claims.

Massachusetts Policy Agenda

11 State Policy Recommendations

This framework responds to the unresolved fallout from Steward while identifying broader vulnerabilities in Massachusetts health care. Its central principle is that patients and individual health care professionals should not be left bearing the consequences of institutional and financial failures they did not create and could not control.

Create a Steward Medical Malpractice Tribunal & Steward-Specific Review Process

Requested immediate state action. Establish a Steward-specific process building on M.G.L. c. 231, §60B, with substantial representation from licensed health care providers and appropriate representation of the professions involved in the care. The process should consider whether a claim presents a legitimate question of liability, whether each person named had an actual role in the alleged harm, and whether systemic failures such as understaffing, resource limitations, institutional decisions, or inaccessible malpractice coverage contributed. Where evidence supports institutional or executive responsibility, that responsibility should be examined rather than shifted automatically to individual clinicians.

Require Steward Entities to Be Addressed in Steward-Related Litigation

Requested immediate state action. PROTECT proposes that Steward Medical Group and other relevant Steward business entities be addressed in litigation arising from Steward operations, rather than allowing institutional conduct to disappear from consideration while individual clinicians remain exposed. Any mechanism should operate consistently with bankruptcy-court orders, procedural rules, and applicable state and federal law.

Establish Emergency Liability Protections for Care Delivered During the Steward Crisis

Modeled on Massachusetts' 2020 COVID emergency legislation. Massachusetts should examine whether the extraordinary conditions surrounding the Steward collapse warrant targeted liability protections for good-faith care delivered amid severe institutional disruption, resource loss, staffing pressure, and other conditions outside individual clinicians' control. The proposal draws on the Commonwealth's 2020 response, when emergency legislation provided qualified civil-liability protections for certain health care services affected by COVID-19 emergency conditions.

Create a Steward Recovery Fund & Steward Malpractice Fund

Requested immediate state action. Create a mechanism to address reported unpaid Steward-related settlements and judgments while preserving the Commonwealth's ability to pursue recovery from Steward, TRACO, insurers, executives, affiliates, or other legally responsible parties. PROTECT also proposes a consolidated malpractice mechanism with fair pricing for affected providers to reduce duplicative individual costs and coordinate resources. This should operate in tandem with the proposed Steward-specific review process.

Address Malpractice Insurance Consequences for Former Steward Clinicians

Former Steward clinicians who believed they had employer-provided malpractice protection may now face substantial costs for tail or replacement coverage. PROTECT calls for review by the Attorney General and Division of Insurance of pricing and market conduct, with refunds or penalties where authorized by existing law and supported by the evidence.

Ensure Accountability for the Steward Collapse

Continue investigation of conduct surrounding Steward's collapse and pursue appropriate civil, criminal, or regulatory remedies where supported by the evidence. PROTECT also calls for meaningful public transparency, to the extent permitted by law, concerning investigative and charging decisions.

Strengthen Oversight of Malpractice Insurance & Reserves

Examine whether assets securing Massachusetts malpractice liabilities should be required to remain domestically accessible, adequately capitalized, transparent, and subject to stronger regulatory oversight. Review captive governance, reserve adequacy, reinsurance, related-party transactions, and mechanisms that ensure promised protection remains reachable when a health system restructures, closes, or enters bankruptcy.

Remove Limits on Hospital Liability

Reconsider the $100,000 limitation in M.G.L. c. 231, §85K for qualifying nonprofit health care organizations. PROTECT proposes that institutional responsibility should be appropriately shared when evidence shows hospital-level decisions involving staffing, budgets, equipment, coverage, or other system-level conditions contributed to patient harm.

Create Meaningful Civil Accountability for Hospital Executives

Examine circumstances in which executives and governing bodies may be held accountable for knowing or reckless decisions that improperly extract assets, undermine financial safeguards, compromise staffing or resources, or contribute to an institution's inability to meet patient-care obligations. Corporate structure should not automatically insulate individuals from responsibility for their own legally actionable conduct.

Modernize PA Practice Laws

Modernize Massachusetts PA law to reduce unnecessary supervisory requirements, align liability more closely with clinicians actually involved in a patient's care, and preserve access when health systems close, restructure, or lose physicians. PROTECT supports consideration of H.2371/S.1502 and related reforms to the statutory supervision framework in M.G.L. c. 112, §9E.

Strengthen Protections for Healthcare Providers Who Raise Quality & Safety Concerns

Providers should be able to raise good-faith concerns about staffing, resources, patient safety, systemic failures, and health care quality with policymakers and regulators without professional retaliation. Massachusetts should examine stronger remedies and penalties for retaliation while preserving legitimate investigation of underlying facts and excluding knowingly false statements or concealment of evidence from protection.

Shared principle: Patients and individual health care professionals should not be left bearing the consequences of institutional and financial failures they did not create and could not control.

Where is the malpractice risk actually held? This directory maps Massachusetts hospital systems and hospitals to publicly identifiable captive insurers, risk-retention arrangements, or other professional-liability structures. This directory includes only organizations for which a captive or comparable professional-liability vehicle and its domicile can be supported by a public source. Federal Policy

Federal Reform: Corporate Control of Medicine

The lessons of Steward extend beyond Massachusetts. PROTECT supports examining federal reforms that protect clinical independence, strengthen accountability, and reduce the risk that financial structures interfere with patient care.

Federal Legislation

Stop Corporate Takeovers of Physicians Act

Introduced in Congress in September 2026, the proposal targets corporate ownership and control arrangements in physician practices, including structures that lawmakers say can allow private-equity firms, insurers, and other for-profit entities to exercise control through management organizations or nominal physician ownership.

Read the Senate Finance Committee summary →

PROTECT Perspective

Why it belongs in this conversation

PROTECT's Massachusetts proposals focus on malpractice coverage, institutional accountability, workforce protection, and access to care. Federal corporate-practice reform addresses a related question: who ultimately controls clinical organizations and the decisions that affect patients and healthcare professionals?

Federal proposals and the nine Massachusetts recommendations are presented separately because they operate under different laws and would require action by different levels of government.

About PROTECT

PROTECT stands for Patients and Providers for Reform, Oversight, Transparency, Equity, and Clinical Trust.

PROTECT is a coalition of patients, healthcare professionals, advocates, and organizations working to strengthen accountability, protect access to care, and ensure that patients and individual healthcare professionals are not left bearing the consequences when healthcare institutions fail.
